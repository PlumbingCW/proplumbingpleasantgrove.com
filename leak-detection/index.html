Warning signs

Signs of a hidden leak

Hidden leaks leave clues long before a stain appears. Any of these is worth a check.

  • A water bill that rises with no change in habits.
  • A water meter that moves when every fixture is off.
  • Warm spots on a slab floor or a hot water line that never cools.
  • Musty smell, peeling paint or bubbling drywall.
  • Sounds of running water in a quiet house.
  • A green, wet patch in the yard with no rain or sprinklers.
The test

The meter test you can do yourself

Turn off every fixture and appliance, then check the leak indicator on your water meter. A moving dial or spinning triangle means water is passing somewhere. Wait half an hour and look again, then note whether the change is steady.

If the meter moves, close the main valve at the house and check again. If it still moves, the leak is in the line between the meter and the house, and a plumber can locate it with acoustic gear.

Slab leaks

Slab leaks and under-house leaks

A leak in a hot or cold line under a concrete slab can run for months. Warm patches in a floor, a bill that jumps and sounds of water in walls are the common signs. We pinpoint the spot with listening gear, then discuss spot repair, rerouting the line through the attic or walls, or repiping.

Homes with a crawl space allow direct access to supply lines. In those cases a leak is often easier to find and fix, though a damp crawl space can invite mold if left alone.

Outdoors

Yard, irrigation and pool leaks

Utah summers push irrigation hard, and a broken sprinkler line or a failed valve can waste water and run up a bill fast. A buried service line between the meter and house is another common site. We trace the leak by pressure testing sections and listening along the line path.

Pool and spa lines get one test only: a pressure isolation check that tells whether the leak is in the plumbing or the shell. A shell leak belongs with a pool specialist.

Why it matters

A small leak wastes a lot of water

The U.S. EPA WaterSense program estimates that the average household loses nearly 10,000 gallons of water a year to leaks. A toilet flapper, a dripping faucet or a buried line can each account for a share, which is why a meter check and a quiet house test are worth doing even when nothing looks wet.

What to do

What to do the moment you find a leak

Finding the source is the plumber's job, but you can limit the harm right away.

  • Close the main water valve, then open a low tap to drain pressure.
  • Turn off power to wet areas only if the panel is dry.
  • Do not cut into a wall or slab yourself, since you may hit a live line.
  • Mop and dry wet materials within a day to prevent mold.
  • Keep a record of the meter reading and the date for your insurer.
