Is a Tankless Water Heater Good in Pleasant Grove, UT?

A tankless water heater is a good fit for many Pleasant Grove homes, especially those that use less hot water than a family of six and plan to stay a decade. It is not automatically cheaper over its life, and cold winters, hard water and gas line size all change the math. The right answer depends on your household.

Here is how to decide.

By the Pleasant Grove Pro Plumbing teamPublished November 14, 2026Updated November 14, 2026
The tradeoff

What a tankless heater does differently

A tank heater keeps forty to fifty gallons hot all day, which wastes some energy as standby loss. A tankless unit heats water only when you open a tap, so it avoids standby loss and can run longer without running out. The price is a higher upfront cost and more demanding gas and venting.

Savings depend on how much hot water you use. A small household saves a little each month. A big household may see less saving because the unit works hard for longer.

Cold winters

How Utah winters change the numbers

A tankless unit raises water temperature by a fixed amount at a given flow. In winter, incoming water is colder, so the same unit delivers less hot water at the same temperature. A unit sized for summer can feel weak in January.

Ask your plumber to size the unit for winter conditions, and consider a model with a higher output than a summer calculation suggests. Two showers at once, plus a dishwasher, is the test most households should plan for.

Good fit

Signs tankless suits your home

These are the households where tankless tends to pay off.

  • A household of one to four people with steady, not heavy, hot water use.
  • A home with limited space for a tank.
  • A gas line that can support the unit, or a plan to upgrade it.
  • A plan to stay in the home for ten years or more.
  • A willingness to descale the unit once a year.
Hard water

Scale is the hidden cost

Utah County water carries dissolved minerals, and scale builds inside a tankless heat exchanger. Without flushing, scale cuts flow and efficiency and can end the unit early. Most manufacturers call for annual descaling, and warranty terms often require it.

A softener or filter can reduce scale, though it adds its own cost. See water softener and filtration for how to test first, and tankless water heater service for yearly descaling.

Local note

Local conditions to check

Pleasant Grove homes vary widely in age, and older homes often have a gas line sized for a tank heater. A tankless unit may need a larger line, and the gas utility and a licensed plumber must confirm capacity. Pleasant Grove also requires permits for most water heater work, so ask the building department what applies.

Cost

What it costs to switch

A tankless unit costs more than a tank, and the install can cost more if the gas line or vent must change. Rebates may exist from the gas utility or the manufacturer, and they change often, so confirm current offers. The payback period is often longer than sales brochures say.

If your tank is failing now and you need hot water fast, water heater replacement with a tank is quicker. If you can plan, a tankless install in a home near Highland or Cedar Hills follows the same process described on our Highland and Cedar Hills pages.

The honest numbers

Why savings are often smaller than advertised

Marketing often cites large savings compared with an old tank. Against a modern, well insulated tank, the gap is smaller, and the extra cost of a tankless unit and its install can take many years to repay. A household that uses little hot water saves the least in dollars, even though it benefits from the convenience.

Run the math with your own gas bill. Look at the share that goes to water heating, estimate a realistic percentage saving, and compare it with the added install cost. If the payback is long, choose tankless for its other benefits and not for savings alone.

Questions for your plumber

What to ask before you commit

A short conversation before the quote prevents surprises on install day.

  • Does my gas line need to be upsized, and what will that add to the quote.
  • Where will the vent run, and does it meet clearance rules.
  • What flow does this model deliver at winter incoming water temperatures.
  • What yearly service does the maker require to keep the warranty.
  • What is the lead time for parts if something fails.
Who should wait

Cases where a tank is still the better choice

A tank heater is the better choice for some homes. If your gas line is small and upsizing it would cost a lot, or if there is no practical place to vent a tankless unit, a tank avoids those costs. A large household with many showers at once may also do better with a tank or with two tankless units.

If you plan to sell within a few years, you may not recover the added cost of a tankless unit. Buyers often value a working heater, not the type. Weigh your time in the house before you decide.

Bottom line

How to decide in ten minutes

Write down your household size, your gas bill share for water heating and how long you plan to stay. Then ask a plumber for two quotes, a tank and a tankless, with venting and gas work included. Compare the extra cost with a realistic yearly saving.

If the tankless premium pays back within your time in the house and your site suits it, go ahead. If not, a good tank is a sound choice.
